# The Orchard of Orra

The Orchard of Orra grows on a broad hill where the keepers cultivate ordinary fruit and one ceremonial pear each year. The pear is harvested by a named custom, and the orchard recognizes two customs as equally valid. A harvester chooses one complete custom before climbing. The keepers dislike mixtures because a mixed sequence belongs to neither tradition.

## How the customs began

The old custom was established by Orra's first gardeners. A harvester climbs the eastern ladder, places the pear in a silver basket, and waits for the noon bell before carrying it down. The silver basket is kept in the tool house and is used only for ceremonial fruit. The old custom is quiet and measured, and many families continue to teach it.

The newer custom came from traveling orchardists who preferred a flexible climb. A harvester climbs the western ladder, catches the pear in a linen sling, and listens for the evening whistle before leaving the hill. The linen sling hangs beside the western shed. It is a fully recognized custom, not an inferior substitute, and the keepers record both customs in their yearly ledger.

## The two complete customs

The old custom is performed in this order: Climb the eastern ladder. Place the pear in the silver basket. Wait for the noon bell. These details belong together, and a traveler using this custom completes all three steps.

The newer custom is performed in this order: Climb the western ladder. Catch the pear in the linen sling. Listen for the evening whistle. These details belong together, and a traveler using this custom completes all three steps.

## Advice from the keeper

The keeper tells visitors to choose one complete custom before beginning. Guides generally direct first-time visitors toward the old custom, while recognizing the other complete custom as valid. The town's records describe both customs without declaring either one morally better, safer, or more successful. A person who follows either whole sequence reaches the stated goal. A person who combines a bridge, object, or destination from different customs is no longer following a recognized procedure.

The surrounding details help explain why the customs remain in use. Older families may remember one set of words, while newer families may remember another. These preferences are ordinary matters of habit, not evidence that one route is universally correct. For a practical answer, name one complete sequence and keep its steps in the order given above.

Visitors sometimes ask whether weather, family history, or personal taste changes the rule. The keeper answers that those details may explain a choice, but they do not change the written sequence. The objects and destinations have unusual names so that a careful reader can tell the customs apart. Nothing outside this tale is needed to decide what a traveler should do.
