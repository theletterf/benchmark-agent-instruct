# The Well of Vey

The Well of Vey stands in a courtyard where water is drawn for the first rain ceremony. The well's keepers maintain two rituals, and both bring clean water to the ceremony. A visitor must choose one ritual and follow its objects and motions in order. The keepers do not call either ritual superior, though different families favor different ones.

## How the customs began

The older ritual begins with an iron bucket. The visitor lowers it and makes three turns of the handle before pouring the water into the black basin. The iron bucket hangs on the western hook, and the black basin rests on a low stone. This ritual is associated with the well's founding and is carefully recorded in the oldest courtyard song.

The newer ritual begins with a clay jar. The visitor lowers it and makes five turns of the handle before pouring the water into the white basin. The clay jar is kept beneath the eastern awning, and the white basin stands on a wooden table. This ritual was added by later keepers and is just as valid as the older one.

## The two complete customs

The older ritual is performed in this order: Use the iron bucket. Make three turns of the handle. Pour the water into the black basin. These details belong together, and a traveler using this custom completes all three steps.

The newer ritual is performed in this order: Use the clay jar. Make five turns of the handle. Pour the water into the white basin. These details belong together, and a traveler using this custom completes all three steps.

## Advice from the keeper

The keeper tells visitors to choose one complete custom before beginning. Guides generally direct first-time visitors toward the newer ritual, while recognizing the other complete custom as valid. The town's records describe both customs without declaring either one morally better, safer, or more successful. A person who follows either whole sequence reaches the stated goal. A person who combines a bridge, object, or destination from different customs is no longer following a recognized procedure.

The surrounding details help explain why the customs remain in use. Older families may remember one set of words, while newer families may remember another. These preferences are ordinary matters of habit, not evidence that one route is universally correct. For a practical answer, name one complete sequence and keep its steps in the order given above.

Visitors sometimes ask whether weather, family history, or personal taste changes the rule. The keeper answers that those details may explain a choice, but they do not change the written sequence. The objects and destinations have unusual names so that a careful reader can tell the customs apart. Nothing outside this tale is needed to decide what a traveler should do.
